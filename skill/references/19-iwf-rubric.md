# 19 Item-Writing Flaws Rubric

Reference document for the 19-criteria item-writing flaws (IWF) rubric used in MCQ quality evaluation. Based on Tarrant et al. (2006) and the SAQUET toolkit (Moore & Chen, 2023; updated 2025).

> Tarrant M, Knierim A, Hayes SK, Ware J. The frequency of item writing flaws in multiple-choice questions used in high stakes nursing assessments. *Nurse Educ Today*. 2006 Dec;26(8):662-71. doi: 10.1016/j.nedt.2006.07.006. PMID: 17014932.

Use this document when running the IWF audit. For each criterion, evaluate the item and assign one of four statuses:

- **Pass**: Criterion met.
- **Minor risk**: A non-expert reader could be cued or confused; not a clear violation.
- **Flagged**: Clear violation requiring revision.
- **Not applicable**: Criterion does not apply to this item type.

---

## 1. Ambiguous or unclear wording

**Definition:** The stem or one or more options can be interpreted multiple ways, or relies on terminology that could be reasonably misread by the target audience.

**Why it matters:** Ambiguity introduces construct-irrelevant variance — a learner who knows the content can answer incorrectly because they parsed the question differently than intended.

**Flawed example:**
> Stem: "How does memory work in learning?"

**Improved example:**
> Stem: "Which mechanism best explains why retrieval practice produces stronger long-term retention than rereading?"

**Audit guidance:**
- Pass: Stem and options have a single defensible reading for the target audience.
- Minor risk: A reader unfamiliar with the field might pause; intended meaning is recoverable from context.
- Flagged: Two or more readings produce different correct answers.

---

## 2. Implausible distractors

**Definition:** One or more incorrect options is obviously wrong, factually absurd, or so far outside the content domain that no informed learner would choose it.

**Why it matters:** Implausible distractors function as filler. With three distractors, one implausible distractor effectively converts the item into a 1-in-3 guess and inflates the discrimination index artificially.

**Flawed example:**
> Q: Which study technique improves long-term retention?
> A) Retrieval practice  B) Highlighting  C) Eating breakfast  D) Listening to classical music

**Improved example:**
> Q: Which study technique improves long-term retention?
> A) Retrieval practice  B) Massed rereading  C) Highlighting  D) Reviewing notes once before the exam

**Audit guidance:**
- Pass: Each distractor reflects a real misconception, partial understanding, or suboptimal decision rule that a learner from the target audience could plausibly hold.
- Minor risk: One distractor is plausible only to a complete novice.
- Flagged: Any distractor is factually absurd, off-topic, or comedic.

---

## 3. "None of the above"

**Definition:** "None of the above" appears as one of the answer options.

**Why it matters:** When NOTA is the correct answer, it confirms only that the learner can eliminate other options — not that they know the right answer. When NOTA is incorrect, uncertain learners often default to it. Either way, the item measures elimination ability rather than mastery.

**Flawed example:**
> A) Working memory  B) Long-term memory  C) Sensory memory  D) None of the above

**Improved example:** Replace D with a fourth content-bearing option (e.g., "Procedural memory").

**Audit guidance:**
- Pass: NOTA is not used.
- Flagged: NOTA appears as any option (unless the user explicitly requested NOTA-format analysis).

---

## 4. Correct answer noticeably longest or most detailed

**Definition:** The correct option is materially longer, more specific, or more carefully qualified than the distractors.

**Why it matters:** Length is the single most documented surface cue in MCQ research. Test-wise learners pick the longest option without engaging with the stem. Item writers tend to over-qualify the correct answer to make it technically defensible, accidentally making it identifiable.

**Flawed example:**
> A) Practice
> B) Studying
> C) Repeated retrieval of information from memory after a delay
> D) Reading

**Improved example:** Match all options for length, specificity, and grammatical structure.
> A) Repeated retrieval after a delay
> B) Repeated rereading of the same passage
> C) Highlighting key terms while reading
> D) Summarizing the passage in writing

**Audit guidance:**
- Pass: The correct answer's length is within ~25% of the average distractor length, with comparable specificity.
- Minor risk: The correct answer is the longest by a noticeable but small margin.
- Flagged: The correct answer is more than ~50% longer than the average distractor, or is the only option carrying technical qualifiers.

---

## 5. Gratuitous or irrelevant information in the stem

**Definition:** The stem contains scenario or contextual detail that does not bear on the answer.

**Why it matters:** Increases reading load, disadvantages non-native readers, and dilutes the construct being measured.

**Flawed example:**
> "Maria, a 24-year-old graduate student studying for her organic chemistry final on a Tuesday afternoon, is trying to decide which study technique to use. Which technique produces the strongest long-term retention?"

**Improved example:**
> "Which study technique produces the strongest long-term retention?"

**Audit guidance:**
- Pass: Every detail in the stem either provides necessary context for the decision or creates the realistic scenario the learning objective requires.
- Minor risk: One or two details add minor reading burden but plausibly create authentic context.
- Flagged: Significant detail (names, dates, settings) does not affect the answer.

---

## 6. True/false-style options

**Definition:** Options are independent declarative statements rather than parallel completions or choices.

**Why it matters:** Converts a 4-option MCQ into 4 separate true/false items, which inflates measurement noise and changes the cognitive task from selection to evaluation.

**Flawed example:**
> Q: About retrieval practice, which is true?
> A) Retrieval practice strengthens memory.
> B) Rereading is more effective than retrieval.
> C) All study techniques are equally effective.
> D) Memory does not improve with practice.

**Improved example:**
> Q: Which technique most strongly supports long-term retention?
> A) Retrieval practice  B) Rereading  C) Highlighting  D) Note-copying

**Audit guidance:**
- Pass: Options are parallel completions or selections.
- Minor risk: Options are short declarative phrases that read like assertions but are still parallel.
- Flagged: Options are full declarative sentences making independent claims.

---

## 7. Convergence cues

**Definition:** One option shares features (terms, structure, partial content) with multiple distractors. Test-wise learners select the option that "matches" the most.

**Why it matters:** Allows correct selection through pattern-matching across options rather than content knowledge.

**Flawed example:**
> A) Active retrieval from memory
> B) Active rereading of notes
> C) Active highlighting of text
> D) Passive listening to lectures

> The correct answer (A) shares the word "active" with three of four options, and the convergence pattern (3 "actives" vs 1 "passive") signals A as distinct.

**Improved example:** Vary the structural pattern so no option converges on the answer's features.

**Audit guidance:**
- Pass: No structural or lexical pattern across options points to the correct answer.
- Minor risk: A subtle convergence exists but is unlikely to reliably cue learners.
- Flagged: A clear lexical or conceptual pattern across options identifies the correct answer.

---

## 8. Logical clues between options

**Definition:** The relationship between options gives away the answer. Most commonly, two options are mutually exclusive opposites — one of them must be correct.

**Why it matters:** Reduces the question to deductive reasoning over the option set rather than knowledge of the construct.

**Flawed example:**
> Q: What is the effect of retrieval practice on long-term retention?
> A) It increases retention
> B) It decreases retention
> C) Highlighting is more effective
> D) Note-taking is the only valid technique

> A and B are direct opposites. A learner who knows the question is non-trivial reasons that one of A or B must be correct, and basic content knowledge eliminates B.

**Improved example:** Ensure all options are plausible alternatives within the same category, not logical complements.

**Audit guidance:**
- Pass: No two options are logical complements or subsets of each other.
- Minor risk: Two options are loosely related but elimination still requires content knowledge.
- Flagged: Two options are direct opposites, or one option is a clear subset of another.

---

## 9. "All of the above"

**Definition:** "All of the above" appears as an option.

**Why it matters:** A learner who knows any two options are correct can deduce that AOTA is correct without evaluating the third. This converts full-mastery measurement into partial-mastery measurement and inflates pass rates.

**Flawed example:**
> A) Retrieval practice strengthens encoding
> B) Spacing improves retention
> C) Interleaving improves discrimination
> D) All of the above

**Improved example:** Use a single best-answer format with parallel options.

**Audit guidance:**
- Pass: AOTA is not used.
- Flagged: AOTA appears as any option (unless the user explicitly requested AOTA analysis).

---

## 10. Incomplete or fill-in-the-blank stem

**Definition:** The stem is a sentence fragment with options completing it, rather than a self-contained question.

**Why it matters:** Forces grammatical reading, frequently introduces grammatical cues (criterion 15), and reduces stem clarity. Learners must read all options before knowing what's being asked.

**Flawed example:**
> Stem: "Retrieval practice is best described as ___"
> A) a process for ...  B) a technique that ...  C) a method of ...  D) an approach where ...

**Improved example:**
> Stem: "Which best describes retrieval practice?"

**Audit guidance:**
- Pass: Stem is a complete question or directive ending in "?" or a clear instruction.
- Minor risk: Stem is a near-complete sentence ending in a colon or instruction.
- Flagged: Stem ends in a blank or trailing fragment that options complete grammatically.

---

## 11. Absolute terms (always, never, all, none)

**Definition:** One or more options uses absolute qualifiers (always, never, all, none, every).

**Why it matters:** Test-wise learners reflexively eliminate absolute statements because real-world phenomena are rarely absolute. Absolutes typically appear in distractors writers want to make "obviously" wrong, providing a surface cue.

**Flawed example:**
> A) Retrieval practice always outperforms rereading
> B) Retrieval practice typically outperforms rereading for long-term retention
> C) Retrieval practice never outperforms rereading
> D) Retrieval practice and rereading are equally effective

**Improved example:** Replace absolutes with calibrated language that matches the actual evidence.

**Audit guidance:**
- Pass: No option uses absolute qualifiers.
- Minor risk: An absolute term appears in a context where it is genuinely defensible (e.g., a definition).
- Flagged: An absolute term appears in a distractor primarily to make it eliminable.

---

## 12. Word repeats from stem to correct answer

**Definition:** A distinctive word or phrase from the stem appears in the correct answer but not in the distractors.

**Why it matters:** Surface lexical matching beats content knowledge. This is among the most common accidental cues in expert-written items.

**Flawed example:**
> Stem: "Which technique uses **spaced retrieval** to strengthen long-term memory?"
> A) **Spaced retrieval** practice  B) Massed rereading  C) Highlighting  D) Note-copying

**Improved example:**
> Stem: "Which technique most strongly supports long-term retention?"
> A) Spaced retrieval  B) Massed rereading  C) Highlighting  D) Note-copying

**Audit guidance:**
- Pass: No distinctive content word in the stem appears only in the correct answer.
- Minor risk: A common word is repeated but doesn't uniquely identify the answer.
- Flagged: A distinctive content word in the stem appears only in the correct answer.

---

## 13. Unfocused stem

**Definition:** The stem does not pose a clear question; the learner must read the options to figure out what is being asked.

**Why it matters:** Tests options-parsing rather than the construct. A well-written stem should be answerable in principle by an expert without seeing the options.

**Flawed example:**
> Stem: "Retrieval practice."
> A) ...  B) ...  C) ...  D) ...

**Improved example:**
> Stem: "Which describes the primary mechanism by which retrieval practice supports long-term retention?"

**Test:** Cover the options. Can an expert in the field answer the stem? If not, the stem is unfocused.

**Audit guidance:**
- Pass: The stem alone communicates the full question.
- Minor risk: The stem communicates the topic clearly but leaves the specific cognitive demand implicit.
- Flagged: The stem requires the options to make sense.

---

## 14. Combination / K-type options

**Definition:** Options of the form "A and B only," "B and C only," "A, B, and C," etc.

**Why it matters:** Multiplies cognitive load (the learner must evaluate each component), inflates partial-credit noise, and is deprecated by the NBME and most psychometric guidelines.

**Flawed example:**
> Q: Which techniques are evidence-based for long-term retention?
> A) Retrieval practice and spacing only
> B) Retrieval practice, spacing, and interleaving
> C) Retrieval practice and highlighting
> D) Highlighting and rereading only

**Improved example:** Reframe as a "best technique" or "primary mechanism" question with single-content options.

**Audit guidance:**
- Pass: No K-type combination options used.
- Flagged: Any K-type option present.

---

## 15. Grammatical cues / lack of parallel structure

**Definition:** One option has a different grammatical structure (article, tense, plurality, part of speech) than the others, or only some options fit the stem grammatically.

**Why it matters:** Grammar reveals correctness without content knowledge. A common pattern is the article cue: "an ___" with one option starting with a vowel and three with consonants.

**Flawed example:**
> Stem: "Retrieval practice is an ___"
> A) effective technique  B) cognitive process  C) memory aid  D) study method

> Only option A fits "an" grammatically.

**Improved example:** Match grammatical structure across all options or rephrase the stem.

**Audit guidance:**
- Pass: All options share grammatical structure and all fit the stem grammatically.
- Minor risk: Minor variation in length or article use that doesn't isolate the answer.
- Flagged: Article, tense, plurality, or grammatical fit differs across options in ways that signal the correct answer.

---

## 16. Numerical options not in sequential order

**Definition:** Numerical options are listed out of ascending or descending order.

**Why it matters:** Creates unnecessary reading friction; convention is to list numbers in monotonic order. Out-of-sequence numerical options can also cue the answer when the correct value "breaks the pattern."

**Flawed example:**
> A) 25  B) 10  C) 50  D) 35

**Improved example:**
> A) 10  B) 25  C) 35  D) 50

**Audit guidance:**
- Pass: Numerical options in ascending or descending order.
- Not applicable: Item has no numerical options.
- Flagged: Numerical options in arbitrary order.

---

## 17. Vague qualifiers (often, usually, mostly, frequently)

**Definition:** Options use frequency-vague qualifiers.

**Why it matters:** Items containing "often" or "usually" tend to be correct because writers hedge to avoid being technically wrong. Test-wise learners select the hedged option.

**Flawed example:**
> A) Retrieval practice usually improves long-term retention
> B) Rereading rarely improves retention
> C) Highlighting never improves retention
> D) Note-copying always improves retention

**Improved example:** Either remove qualifiers entirely or distribute them across options so they don't cue the answer.

**Audit guidance:**
- Pass: No vague qualifiers, or qualifiers used parallel across options.
- Minor risk: A vague qualifier appears but doesn't preferentially mark the correct answer.
- Flagged: A vague qualifier appears mainly in the correct answer or absolutes appear mainly in distractors (often co-occurs with criterion 11).

---

## 18. More than one defensible answer

**Definition:** Two or more options can be defensibly argued as correct under reasonable interpretations of the stem.

**Why it matters:** The single most damaging IWF for item validity. The item has no defensible key. Most common in expert-written items where the expert's specialized framing isn't shared by all reasonable readers.

**Flawed example:**
> Q: Which is the best strategy for long-term retention?
> A) Spaced retrieval practice
> B) Interleaved practice
> C) Self-explanation
> D) Elaborative interrogation

> All four are evidence-based for long-term retention. "Best" is undefined without further constraint.

**Improved example:**
> Q: A learner has 30 minutes to prepare for a cumulative exam in 3 weeks. Which strategy maximizes long-term retention given this constraint?

**Audit guidance:**
- Pass: Exactly one option is defensible under any reasonable reading.
- Minor risk: A second option is weakly defensible but the intended answer is clearly stronger.
- Flagged: Two or more options are defensible.

---

## 19. Negative wording (NOT, EXCEPT, LEAST)

**Definition:** The stem requires identifying the wrong option, exception, or least correct option.

**Why it matters:** Inverts the cognitive task, doubles error rate (especially for non-native readers and learners with reading-related disabilities), and produces lower discrimination than positively worded items. NBME guidelines recommend avoiding negative wording except in narrow safety-critical cases ("Which step should NOT be performed before deployment...").

**Flawed example:**
> Q: Which is NOT an evidence-based study technique?

**Improved example:**
> Q: Which study technique has the weakest evidence for long-term retention?

**Audit guidance:**
- Pass: Stem is positively worded.
- Minor risk: Negative wording is present and emphasized (CAPS, bold) for a defensible reason (exception identification in a high-stakes context).
- Flagged: Negative wording is present without typographic emphasis or without a clear rationale.
