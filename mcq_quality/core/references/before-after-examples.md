# Before/After Examples

Reference these when uncertain how to revise a flagged item. Each example shows a flawed MCQ, the IWF criteria it violates, and a revised version with the fix explained.

---

## Example 1 — Length cue (#4) and implausible distractors (#2)

**Flawed:**

> Which best describes how spaced repetition affects memory?
> A) It helps memory
> B) It hurts memory
> C) The repeated retrieval of information across increasing time intervals strengthens long-term retention by leveraging the spacing effect documented in cognitive psychology research
> D) It has no effect on memory

**Flags:** #4 (option C ~5x longer), #2 (B and D are implausibly oppositional), #8 (A and B are direct logical opposites).

**Revised:**

> Which mechanism best explains how spaced repetition supports long-term retention?
> A) Repeated retrieval at increasing intervals strengthens recall pathways
> B) Repeated rereading consolidates material into long-term storage
> C) Rapid massed practice prevents forgetting before consolidation

**What changed:** All options now describe plausible mechanisms a novice might endorse, parallel in length and structure. The correct answer (A) is no longer identifiable by length or by being the only "real" answer.

## Example 2 — Word repeat (#12)

**Flawed:**

> What cognitive process is engaged during retrieval practice?
> A) Recognition of familiar material
> B) Retrieval of information from long-term memory
> C) Encoding of new information into working memory
> D) Consolidation during sleep

**Flags:** #12 — the word "retrieval" from the stem appears only in the correct answer.

**Revised:**

> What cognitive process most strongly supports long-term retention when practiced repeatedly?
> A) Recognition of familiar material
> B) Active recall from long-term memory
> C) Encoding of new information into working memory
> D) Consolidation during sleep

**What changed:** The stem no longer contains the cuing word. "Retrieval" was rephrased as "active recall" in option B so the answer doesn't pattern-match a stem word.

## Example 3 — Multiple defensible answers (#18)

**Flawed:**

> Which is the best study technique for long-term retention?
> A) Spaced retrieval practice
> B) Interleaved practice
> C) Self-explanation
> D) Elaborative interrogation

**Flags:** #18 — all four are evidence-backed for long-term retention. "Best" is undefined.

**Revised:**

> A learner has 30 minutes per day for three weeks to prepare for a cumulative exam. Which study approach best supports retention of the full exam content?
> A) Spaced retrieval practice across all topics
> B) Massed rereading of the most difficult chapter
> C) Highlighting key terms in the textbook each day
> D) Watching recorded lectures at 1.5x speed

**What changed:** Added scenario constraint (time, scope) that makes one answer clearly best. Replaced same-tier alternatives with a mix of evidence-backed and weaker techniques so the correct answer is unambiguous.

## Example 4 — Absolutes (#11) and vague qualifiers (#17)

**Flawed:**

> Which statement about retrieval practice is most accurate?
> A) Retrieval practice always outperforms rereading
> B) Retrieval practice usually outperforms rereading for delayed retention
> C) Retrieval practice never outperforms rereading
> D) Retrieval practice and rereading are equally effective in all contexts

**Flags:** #11 (absolutes in A, C, D), #17 ("usually" cues correct answer), #6 (true/false-style options).

**Revised:**

> A student studying for an exam two weeks away wants to maximize retention on test day. Which technique is best supported by research?
> A) Spaced retrieval practice across the two weeks
> B) Concentrated rereading the night before
> C) Highlighting the most important passages each day
> D) Copying notes verbatim during each study session

**What changed:** Reframed as a scenario question with parallel option structure. No absolutes, no hedging qualifiers, no true/false-style independent claims.

## Example 5 — Negative wording (#19)

**Flawed:**

> Which of the following is not an evidence-based strategy for long-term retention?
> A) Spaced retrieval practice
> B) Highlighting key terms in a textbook
> C) Interleaving topics during practice
> D) Self-explanation while reading

**Flags:** #19 — negative wording without typographic emphasis or safety-critical justification.

**Revised:**

> Which strategy has the weakest research support for long-term retention?
> A) Spaced retrieval practice
> B) Highlighting key terms in a textbook
> C) Interleaving topics during practice
> D) Self-explanation while reading

**What changed:** Inverted to positive framing. Same correct answer (B) but the cognitive task is now identifying weakest rather than identifying the exception.

## Example 6 — All of the above (#9) and K-type (#14)

**Flawed:**

> Which techniques are supported by research on retrieval-based learning?
> A) Spaced practice and interleaving only
> B) Self-testing and elaborative interrogation
> C) Spaced practice, interleaving, and self-testing
> D) All of the above

**Flags:** #9 (AOTA), #14 (K-type combinations).

**Revised:**

> A learner wants to improve long-term retention. Which technique has the strongest research support?
> A) Spaced retrieval practice
> B) Highlighting passages while reading
> C) Rereading the chapter twice
> D) Watching summary videos

**What changed:** Reframed as single-best-answer with content-bearing options. No combination logic required of the learner.

## Example 7 — Incomplete stem (#10) and grammatical cue (#15)

**Flawed:**

> Retrieval practice is an ___
> A) effective technique for strengthening long-term memory
> B) cognitive process used during encoding
> C) memory aid that works through repetition
> D) study method based on rereading

**Flags:** #10 (fill-in-blank stem), #15 ("an" only fits option A grammatically), #4 (option A is also longest).

**Revised:**

> Which best describes retrieval practice?
> A) A technique for strengthening long-term memory through repeated recall
> B) A cognitive process that occurs primarily during initial encoding
> C) A memory aid that works by repeated exposure to material
> D) A study method based on multiple rereadings of the same text

**What changed:** Stem is a complete question. All options are full noun phrases of similar length and structure. No grammatical cue.

## Example 8 — Gratuitous information (#5) and unfocused stem (#13)

**Flawed:**

> Maria, a 24-year-old second-year graduate student in molecular biology, has a final exam in three weeks on Tuesday morning at 9 AM in Room 204. She has been studying inconsistently throughout the semester and is now feeling anxious about her preparation. Her roommate suggests several techniques. Study techniques.
> A) Spaced retrieval practice across the three weeks
> B) Massed rereading the night before
> C) Highlighting the textbook
> D) Copying lecture notes verbatim

**Flags:** #5 (extensive irrelevant detail), #13 (stem ends with a fragment, not a question).

**Revised:**

> A student has three weeks to prepare for a cumulative final exam. Which approach best supports retention through the exam date?
> A) Spaced retrieval practice across the three weeks
> B) Massed rereading the night before the exam
> C) Highlighting the textbook each day
> D) Copying lecture notes verbatim each evening

**What changed:** Stripped name, age, time, location, emotional state, and roommate framing. Only the time-to-exam constraint (which actually affects the answer) is retained. Stem ends with a clear question.

## Example 9 — Convergence cues (#7)

**Flawed:**

> Which study approach produces the strongest long-term retention?
> A) Active retrieval from memory
> B) Active rereading of notes
> C) Active highlighting of text
> D) Passive listening to lectures

**Flags:** #7 — three options share "active." The pattern (3 actives + 1 passive) cues attention to the structural odd-one-out, but the correct answer is among the "actives," creating a misleading convergence.

**Revised:**

> Which study approach produces the strongest long-term retention?
> A) Recalling material from memory after a delay
> B) Rereading notes immediately after a lecture
> C) Highlighting key passages in a textbook
> D) Listening to recorded lectures at high speed

**What changed:** Removed the repeated "Active/Passive" structural pattern. Each option now describes its mechanism directly. No convergence pattern remains.

## Example 10 — None of the above (#3) and numerical out of order (#16)

**Flawed:**

> Research suggests the optimal spacing interval between study sessions for retention over one week is approximately:
> A) 24 hours
> B) 6 hours
> C) 48 hours
> D) None of the above

**Flags:** #3 (NOTA), #16 (24, 6, 48 — out of ascending order).

**Revised:**

> Research suggests the optimal spacing interval between study sessions for one-week retention is approximately:
> A) 6 hours
> B) 24 hours
> C) 48 hours
> D) 96 hours

**What changed:** NOTA replaced with a fourth content-bearing numerical option. All numbers in ascending order.
